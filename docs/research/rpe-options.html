Measuring player effort in PASS

The options for working out effort and training load from player ratings (RPE). Your choices decide what we build.
Prepared November 2026 · For review by the PASS sports expert

How to read this. Section 1 explains RPE in plain words. Section 2 lists the information the app can collect. Section 3 has nine decisions. Each one gives the options with their pros and cons, and marks the one we suggest with SUGGESTED. These are only suggestions, and the choice is yours. The last page is a checklist where you tick your choices.

The short version

  • After every session the app asks each player "How hard was that session?" and they answer from 1 to 10. That answer is the RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion).
  • The method used most around the world, by professional clubs, national teams and researchers, is called session RPE. It multiplies the rating by the session's length in minutes to give one number, the training load.
  • Everything else (weekly totals, warnings, recovery) is built on top of that number.
  • The app can already collect what this method needs. What we need from you: how to ask the question, which numbers to calculate from the answers, and when to warn players or coaches.
  • How load, soreness and sleep combine into a recovery level is entirely your call (Decision 9).

1. What is RPE?

RPE stands for Rating of Perceived Exertion: how hard the player felt they worked. It's a score from the player's own judgement, not a measurement from a device. It has been tested in dozens of studies across many sports and agrees well with heart rate and other lab measures. It's cheap, quick and works for everyone, which is why it's used so widely.

The standard scale (Foster's 0–10 scale)

Most teams use this version. Each number comes with a word that helps players answer the same way every time.

ScoreMeaningScoreMeaning
0Rest6(between hard and very hard)
1Very, very easy7Very hard
2Easy8(between)
3Moderate9(between)
4Somewhat hard10Maximal, the hardest possible
5Hard

From a rating to a training load

Training load = effort rating × minutes of the session

Example: a player rates a 60-minute session as 6. Their load for that session is 6 × 60 = 360 load points. The load points have no unit. They only mean something when compared with the same player's other sessions and weeks.

So a long easy session and a short hard one can come out with similar loads: 90 minutes at 3 = 270, and 45 minutes at 6 = 270. That's intended, because both put a similar total demand on the body.

2. What the app can collect

The app already knows
When each session was booked to start and end
When each player actually checked in (so lateness and no-shows are known)
The sport, and whether it was play (a match or casual game) or training (with a coach)
Which coach and which team, if any
The player's effort rating (1–10) after the session, plus the minutes, which they can correct
Sore body areas (16 areas, each 0–10) in the morning, in the evening and after sessions
Sleep start and end times, typed in or read from the phone's health app
The app does NOT know (yet)
Heart rate during the session
GPS distance or speed
Training done outside the app (gym, running, other clubs)
Sleep quality (only how long)
Fatigue, stress or mood
Age group or level, if you want different rules for juniors or beginners

Anything in this column can be added if you decide it matters. Some items are easy (a question in the app) and some take more work (reading heart rate from a watch).

Something that helps us: players can only watch their session's video after they've given their effort rating. So we expect far more answers than most clubs get, and missing ratings will be less of a problem.

MonotonyHow samey the week was. Hard days with rest days give a low score, which is good. Similar sessions every day with no rest give a high score.Foster linked high monotony (above about 2) combined with high load to illness and overtraining.

9The recovery level: how do load, soreness and sleep combine?

This is entirely your decision, and we haven't designed anything for it. The app has three ingredients: training load (from RPE), soreness (16 body areas, 0–10, morning, evening and after sessions) and sleep length. Common approaches in sport are:

ApproachHow it worksGoodBad
A. Traffic lightSimple rules. For example, red if soreness is high and sleep is short.Very easy for players to understand.Loses detail. The rules are hand-picked.
B. Score out of 100Each ingredient adds or removes points, with weights you choose.One clear number, easy to track.The weights need your expertise.
C. Compared with their own normal"Your soreness and sleep are worse than your usual." Same idea as Decision 6.Personal and fair.Needs a few weeks of data per player.
Questions for you: Do we need more check-in questions, such as fatigue, stress, mood or sleep quality? (These are common daily 1–5 questions in professional sport.) Should morning soreness count more than evening soreness? How quickly should a hard session "fade" from the recovery level?

5. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing players with each other. One player's "6" isn't the same as another's. Compare each player with themselves over time.
  • Changing the method partway through. A new scale, a new question time or a new minutes rule breaks the comparison with older data. Decide once, then keep it.
  • Treating a missing rating as a rest day. See Decision 5.
  • Trusting one number. No single figure, including the ratio, predicts injury on its own. Look at load, soreness and sleep together.
  • Players copying each other. Ratings should be given privately on each player's own phone, which the app already does.
  • Judging too early. Personal comparisons need about 4 weeks of data before they mean anything.
